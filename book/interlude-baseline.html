Interlude

Do We Need Another System?

Baseline Check

Before inventing anything new, we should make one serious attempt not to.

Mars already has precise coordinates, mature planetary mapping, recognised feature names and software capable of representing almost any boundary we might care to draw. If that existing stack already satisfies the design brief, another planetary system would be unnecessary complexity dressed up as architecture.

So this is a pause rather than another chapter.

We have ten requirements.

How much of the job is already done?

Coordinates are very hard to beat

Coordinates are the strongest part of the existing system.

They are anchored to the measured planet, cover the surface, can become as precise as an application requires and work exceptionally well for machines. Two organisations do not need to share a government, language or database before they can agree that the same latitude and longitude describes the same physical point. They can also use coordinates to define arbitrary polygons, corridors and irregular boundaries rather than merely individual locations.

That is already an impressive answer to several of our requirements.

Coordinates are neutral. They are open. They are deterministic when the reference frame and conventions are specified. They can describe almost any geometry future Mars might need.

If the problem were simply where is this?, there would be little reason to continue the book.

The gap appears when we ask a different question: which persistent piece of Mars is this part of?

Adding decimal places makes a point more precise. It does not automatically place that point inside a shared small area, inside a recognised larger area, inside a still larger one. Coordinates give us exceptionally good measurement; they do not choose the civil units through which different organisations might repeatedly group, compare and discuss that measurement.

That is outside their job description.

Names solve a different problem

Planetary nomenclature adds something coordinates deliberately lack: human language.

A name such as Olympus Mons or Jezero is easy to speak, remember, search and connect with scientific history. Names compress a great deal of geographic context into a small amount of language, which is why the established Martian nomenclature should remain valuable long after permanent settlement begins.

Its selectivity is part of what makes it useful.

Important physical features receive names because people need to refer to them. Ordinary ground may remain unnamed because nobody has any reason to call it anything. Named features also vary enormously in scale and do not form a complete, non-overlapping hierarchy covering every point on Mars.

Again, that is not a failure. It is simply another job.

Coordinates tell us where.

Nomenclature tells us what important physical features are called.

Together, they already provide an extremely capable planetary geography.

What is actually missing?

The interesting result is not that Mars’s existing systems perform badly.

They do not.

Most of the foundations are excellent. Coordinates dominate where we care about physical anchoring, precision, flexible geometry and machine use. Nomenclature contributes human context, continuity and memorable language. Put the two together with modern geospatial software and there is remarkably little about Mars that cannot already be represented.

That substantially raises the burden of proof for Matlas.

A new layer cannot justify itself merely by being easier to draw on a poster. It needs to provide something the existing stack does not already provide naturally.

The candidate gap is narrow:

a shared, exhaustive and persistent system of spatial identities, organised coherently across useful scales.

That is the middle layer introduced in Chapter 2:

measurement → persistent spatial identity → governance and culture

Measurement already exists.

The richer legal, political and cultural geography should be allowed to emerge later.

The question is whether a thin structural layer between them would make a growing Martian civilisation easier to operate without trying to replace either side. The Second-Draft brief deliberately assigns that narrow role to Matlas rather than asking it to become the master geography for everything.

It may not be necessary. A small early settlement could operate perfectly well with coordinates, existing feature names and local conventions, and many probably will. Waiting and allowing every settlement to invent its own system is a legitimate alternative.

But waiting is also a design choice. Once hundreds of local conventions have become embedded in software, records, contracts and daily habit, adding a common layer later becomes considerably harder.

So we have reached a narrower and more useful conclusion than “Mars needs a new map”.

Mars already has excellent measurement and excellent nomenclature. Neither was designed to be an operating geography.

There is no failed system to replace.

There is only a possible layer to add.
